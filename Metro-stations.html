Reminder: these rankings are inherently subjective and take only vibes into account.

The way light filters into Angrignon is otherworldly. The way the bright red of the 1960s-esque arches contrasts with the sky, no matter whether it is the most saturated blue of June or the most leaden grey of November never fails to bring a smile to my face.

The helical flow of traffic around several vertical stories of ultra-saturated stained glass at Charlevoix is beautiful.

The truncated conical skylight filtering over the stairs and down to turnstile level, the 1920s-reminiscent light fixtures built into the walls, and the tiled arches give Outremont a beautifully dignified sense of drama.

The soft pastels of the stained glass at du Collège casts a welcome glow over even the most mundane cloudy days.

Radisson is a hulking concrete cave, a temple to brutalism, and a bit eerie. But these factors coalesce to create the impression of being in the hold of a huge ship rather than on a metro platform. It is haunting in a way that has been growing on me.

The cross-sections of the tunnels at Snowdon are round-cornered rectangles. This shape and the warm brown tiles give the station a cozy feeling.

Vendôme is halfway between ground-level and underground; inhabits the border between NDG, Westmount, and Saint-Henri; and houses a commuter rail interchange. The natural light filtering into this station is a bit less epic than that entering some of the stations I've already discussed, but so many aspects of Vendôme enhance its overarching impression of intense liminality.

Préfontaine looks like LEGO mixed with Révolution Tranquille experimentation. Its primary colours might seem abrasive in isolation, but the light reaching the platforms, leaf-recalling skylights, and ventilation shafts worked into the architectural forms give the station a lovely organic quality.

The forms of Jolicœur are fairly straightforward and boxy, but the light reaching the platforms is consistently ethereal whenever my journey takes me through here.

De Castelnau feels like a step into a different world. Its overlapping concrete trapezoids, agricultural scenes, and sleek wooden benches built into the walls give the impression of peering back in time and into the Eastern Bloc.

The illuminated geometric solids suspended overhead at Namur contrast magnificantly with the sombre walls. This contrast is uplifting, in an everyday and tiny way.

The lattice of three-dimensional rhombuses at Jarry bring light from within each ceiling-bound niche. The tunnels have the cookie-cutter banana bread slice cross- section, but this functional artwork gives the station wonderful character.

Bonaventure feels a bit claustrophobic with its warm, earth-toned tiles and relatively narrow passages. But the impression is nice, on the whole, especially after considering how these tunnels are merely one set of paths in the bowels of the intersection of VIA Rail, Exo, REM, and metro trains, with the core of downtown flowing overhead.

Place-des-Arts can be a maze to navigate. But the experience of taking a two-minute walk directly from the theatre to the metro is unmatched. The sawtooth niches along the platforms are reminiscent of backstage wing access points, a fitting nod to the station's surroundings.

De L'Église has some pleasant concrete textures on the walls, and the impression of verticality while navigating the station is also quite fun.

LaSalle feels like a concrete-and-vinyl gem protruding from the earth. Natural light reaches the platforms, and the contrast between the quiet residential surroundings and comparatively loud station architecture is noteworthy.

Place-d'Armes is fairly everyday on the surface, but its skylights and connections to the RÉSO make it a pleasantly seamless square in the patchwork of the city.

Lionel-Groulx can feel loud—in terms of acoustics, contrasting colours, and general chaos—but the multi-level open-concept design makes it feel calmer. By the time you've carved it into your mental map, the rivers of people changing lines and connecting with the 747 have a comfortable logic in their flows.

Jean-Talon is labyrinthine, full of energy at any time of day, and plagued with low ceilings in some places. The loud murals on the walls above the platform edges are the station's typical claim to fame, but my favourite detail is geometric colour-coordinated floor tiles that point to the Orange and Blue lines.

The glowing orange triangles and patterns of circular lights on the walls at Henri- Bourassa give it a coherent calmness to counterbalance the hubub of people negotiating train transfers and the bus terminal above. Additionally, the experience of getting off the metro, walking through the park, and catching a free concert sponsored by the Maison de Culture Ahuntsic feels quietly utopian.

The window decals at Champ-de-Mars provide a light source, even if the platforms themselves are fairly generic and devoid of natural light.

The blues and reds of Édouard-Montpetit contribute a pleasant, if fairly typical, addition to the concrete story of the Blue line. The round édicule and seamless connection to the futuristic, deep REM station make it stand out, however.

The colours of Parc are fairly loud, but their varied juxtaposition as the train leaves the station is a nice touch, as is the pyramidal skylight and converted-bank édicule.

Villa-Maria makes a bold, yet simple statement in red, orange, yellow, and purple stripes.

Verdun blends cold, vertical, angular, brutalist concrete with purple, orange, and white accents that uplift it.

Crémazie has an impressively intricate mosaic that is abstract, but variously reminiscent of the inside of a lung, a tree, a mathematical network or graph, or some new metaphor that crosses my mind as I pass through.

Côte-des-Neiges has nice édicules on both sides of the street and its location right next to Marché Jean-Billant is particularly convenient. But, the platforms and interior of the station are fairly nondescript.

Monk is deep, and the metal stick figure statues give it character, but the overall impression is a bit drab and sleepy.

Mont-Royal has similar cozy warm-toned tiles and rounded-rectangle tunnel cross-sections as Snowdon, but the effect is less pronounced due to the station's relatively simpler geometry.

Fabre speaks loudly in neons, but the patterns and juxtaposition are memorable and cheerful.

Côte-Sainte-Catherine has platform benches reminiscent of what LEGO minifigures might sit on. Their neon yellow is echoed throughout the station to create a harmonious impression of whimsy.

Université-de-Montréal is punctuated by a refrain of fascinatingly mosaiced terra cotta tiles.

Laurier feels a bit grey and boring, but the unexpectedly saturated reds of the tiles near one stairwell make it stand out.

The crowd-control lanes by the entrance feel a bit spooky on non-event days, but the novelty of an almost-aboveground station is welcome.

Pie-IX has a beautiful diversity of concrete textures. The west-facing exit doors fit into an almost entirely transparent wall that gleams in the oblique sunshine of a bright winter day.

Saint-Michel isn't too architecturally exciting at platform level, but the clear lines of sight between escalator and stair levels is nice.

Place-Saint-Henri feels cozy with warm accent tiles and a penne pasta–shaped lamp, despite the cavernous vertical volume that extends from platform to entrance.

Viau is shallow, but the impression is almost one of being deep underwater with the blue-and-bronze tiles by the entrance.

McGill has some nice local references in its murals, but is particularly crowded, especially after the opening of the A4–Deux Montagnes REM branch.

After spending a couple years living uphill from Guy-Concordia, I quickly learned that the station is certainly not as scary as people make it out to be. My appreciation for the impressive variety of regional grocery stores and restaurants nearby lives on, but I find the red-yellow-black-white tile scheme a bit jarring.

Lucien-L'Allier feels refreshingly everyday and chill, in contrast with its comparatively aloof Petit-Bourgogne–Griffintown–downtown surroundings. Still, it's not as memorable as some of its sibling stations on the network.

The brick-coloured wall tiles at platform level at Beaubien are nice, but something about the station as a whole feels slightly generic and impersonal.

Rosemont feels rectangular, cavernous, and sterile, even if the warm tiles are pleasant.

de la Savane has some nice concrete textures echoed in the spiky wall art hanging overhead, but the relatively empty, suburban surroundings meant this station took a little while to grow on me.

Assomption occupies similar territory in my mind to Langelier: the art seems to fight the station rather than extending its forms. Maybe there are just altogether too many stations adorned with colourful concentric circles in the eastern reaches of the Green line for my taste.

Berri-UQAM is redeemed by its direct connection to the BANQ, but the layout is overall not very exciting and can be a little convoluted to navigate.

The saturated blues, reds, and blacks of Acadie make it look like a piece of experiential concept art for a space station. I appreciate the creativity, and the forms and volumes are memorable, but the colour scheme strikes me as a bit abrasive. I am quite fond of the cube-based skylight near the Campus MIL exit, however.

Longueuil–Université-de-Sherbrooke feels sterile in the manner of an airport or the secondary train station of a midsized European city. Props for connectivity, but the architecture feels a bit soulless.

de la Concorde has a pleasant inclined multi-panel skylight, but an overall hollow and empty impression.

Sherbrooke has a nice purple ceiling, but the boxiness makes the station not so interesting overall.

Georges-Vanier coughs up its passengers into a no-man's land between neighbourhoods where most passengers are better served by another station. The concrete multi-lamppost is fun, though.

Plamondon is okay, but the red-white-blue plastic benches make it feel dated in a vaguely stuffy way or almost reminiscent of a barbershop pole.

Beaudry is saved from the bottom of the list by its inclined moving walkway, but the station is overall a bit drab and not my favourite.

Despite the hypnotic oval-on-rectangle wall tiles contributing an artistic touch, Cadillac feels more "purpose first, art second" than many other metro stations. Of course, I'm glad the metro was made accessible in this part of town, but this station is less impressive to me than so many of the others where fucntion and art work so effectively in conjunction.

Saint-Laurent is a convenient conduit, so I'll somewhat excuse the nondescriptness. The simple out-and-back platform layout is a refreshing change from some of the more intricate stations, I guess.

Honoré-Beaugrand has a beautifully brutalist édicule, but the platforms are totally generic.

d'Iberville tried to make some fun tile shapes, but the impression is not as concerted and impressive as at various other stations

Joliette has some cheerful illuminated glass art near the entrance, but the yellow platform walls tend to get a bit dingy.

Atwater is liminal in a vaguely creepy way and half a story underneath a mall.

Cartier is quintessentially boxy and soulless for a Laval metro station.

Papineau has nice murals, but the tile colour scheme is a bit dissonant to me.

Frontenac has a nice édicule, but the platforms are nondescript.

Sauvé has a generic belowground experience and boring broad intersection at the entrance.

Côte-Vertu has an édicule that could be a municipal office or something.

Square Victoria–OACI has the Paris entrance gimmick and connects to the most generic part of downtown. That's enough for the vibes to be off for me.

Montmorency has some nice pastel diagonally staggered tiles on the platforms, but the edge-of-town cavernousness is strong.

Peel has fun rotated-circle tile murals, but its connections to the RÉSO and overall impression is a little drab.

Langelier has some nice concrete, but this effect is secondary to the cold east-end-of- Green-line vibe.
