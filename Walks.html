Parc René-Lévesque is a world unto itself. On a crisp May morning when I lived in Côte-des-Neiges, I walked there from my place, watching Westmount fade into NDG, Montréal-Ouest, Côte-Saint-Paul, and the Lachine Canal path. The horizon opened up into a beautiful cacophony: statues of human calves built from rebar and boulders, barbeques narrated in ten languages, ancient rapids, and, finally, the end of the land. I make a pilgrimage out here every once in a while, and every time, the water gleams in a new way.

Vieux-Rosemont, particularly Rue Masson. As much as the avant-garde ads and postmodern street art of the Mile-End and Plateau make me secretly smile, corners of the city like these feel refreshingly down to earth.

Rue Wellington and the variously-named riverside park that eventually turns into Parc des Rapides certainly get enough foot traffic to disqualify them from being hidden gems, but they both make the trek west to Verdun more than worthwhile.

Belvédère Outremont is a peaceful place to journal. Its panoramic view without the crowds of Belvédère Kondiaronk are refreshing, and the steep informal footpath down to the old UdeM campus and Edouard-Montpetit metro are charming.

As rarified as the air in Outremont can feel, zig-zagging down Bernard-Ouest—pedestrianized in the summer—or Laurier-Ouest, Fairmount, and Saint-Viateur are still an interesting linearly independent element of the vibe basis of Montreal.

The vicinity of Station Snowdon is noteworthy, even if only for the incredible variety of regional grocery stores in a small area (check out that page for more info!). The length of Queen Mary is worth a walk, as well.

Ahuntsic is the butt of far too many jokes. Promenade Fleury is quieter than many of its counterparts in other boroughs, but this imbues it with a lovely sense of peace. I would also be remiss to omit Île-de-la-Visitation, a mere stroll or bus ride from the metro (Saint-Michel or Henri-Bourassa).

I defend Villeray quite similarly. Its gardens overflow with echinacea in July, sunflowers in August, and dusty tones in September. Its rectangular blocks make it archetypically Montreal. It is at once quaint and happening—this might sound like an oxymoron, but walk down Jarry or Chambord some afternoon and decide for yourself.

Côte-des-Neiges is a symphony extolling the quiet epicness of Montreal. Emerging uphill from the enthralling chaos of the Concordia area, flying past a quintessential midcentury-modern-yet-vaguely-brutalist concrete apartment building at the intersection with Docteur-Penfield that exudes the ethos of La Revolution Tranquille, just past Cedar you reach a panoramic view stretching far past le Rive-Sud. Sure, there's a sleepy stretch in the middle, but the tempo picks back up around the hulking concrete apartment blocks near Queen Mary. The rectangular gridded part is a whirlwind of languages and sights extending until the Jean-Talon train underpass.

Well-known though it may be, running down the length of Mont-Royal, including the discontiguous part east of Saint-Michel in Angus is a fascinating slice of the city that transitions seemingly adiabatically from over-polished to everyday to eerily suburban.

One loop very much on the tourist trail but nonetheless interesting is to metro to Jean-Drapeau, walk around the park, walk across the bridge and Habitat 67, and then either connect to the Lachine Canal paths on foot or take the casino bus back to Station Bonaventure.

I also cannot tolerate Hochelaga slander (are you noticing a theme here?). Check out the libraries page for more sociological musings, but for now I'll just recommend a walk: zigzag from Station Frontenac to the west end of Rue de Rouen, and then follow Rouen to Station Pie-IX. On a summer morning, the gardens make me feel as if I am walking in an oasis of green—almost as if the whole street is an east-west ruelle verte.
